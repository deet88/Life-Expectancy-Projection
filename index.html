⚠ Methodology, Data Sources & Limitations

How the estimate is built

  1. Start from your country's life table. The United Nations' World Population Prospects 2024 gives, for every single year of age and each sex, the probability of dying before the next birthday (qx). The 2024 table is used — the first year of the UN's smooth mortality model rather than the 2023 raw estimate, because the estimates carry artifacts for some countries (Australia's male death rates at ages 21–38 are recorded fifty times too low) and single-year noise for small ones; the two agree on life expectancy at birth to within 0.2 years. Seventeen countries are embedded; the same source and method for all of them, so countries are comparable.
  2. Turn each risk factor into a hazard multiplier. Every factor below comes from a large cohort study or meta-analysis that reports a hazard ratio (HR) — how many times higher the death rate is in one group than in a reference group. The multipliers are combined by multiplication (a proportional-hazards model).
  3. Re-anchor to the population average. A published HR compares you with the study's reference group (never-smokers, BMI 22.5–25…), but a national life table describes the average person, who is a mix of all groups. Applying raw HRs would make almost everyone look worse than average. So for each factor the multiplier actually applied is HRyou ÷ Σ(prevalence × HR) — your ratio to the population-weighted mean. Consequences: a person of exactly average risk reproduces the national table, and a never-smoker correctly lands above the national average. Smoking and BMI use each country's own prevalence (WHO); the other factors use one reference distribution, listed in the table.
  4. Let risk factors fade with age — but not diseases. Every source that reports age strata finds relative risks shrinking at older ages (the BMI collaboration's HR per 5 kg/m² is 1.52 at 35–49 but 1.21 at 70–89; blood-pressure effects are "about half as extreme" at 80–89 as at 40–49; each year of education cuts mortality 2.9% before 50 but 0.8% after 70). The log-HR of each lifestyle, body and social factor is therefore scaled by 1.0 up to age 60, falling linearly to 0.5 twenty-five years later and to 0.25 fifteen years after that. Smoking holds its full strength until 75 (Thun et al. still find ≥ 3× at 55–74). Diagnosed diabetes, heart disease, COPD and kidney disease do not fade: the sources' own life-expectancy figures imply a sustained hazard ratio.
  5. Assume your future looks like the population's, not frozen at today. A 40-year-old without diabetes is not a 40-year-old who will never have diabetes. For each condition you don't have, the chance of developing it by each future age at the population rate is priced into your hazard; blood pressure is assumed to drift upward with age at the median rate (about +17 mmHg from 30 to 70). Without this, a healthy young person is credited with a lifetime free of disease and the estimate runs several years too high.
  6. Recompute survival. The adjusted death rate at each future age gives the survival curve; life expectancy is its area (plus half a year for deaths spread through the final year); the median, the milestone probabilities and the age-at-death distribution all read straight off it. Ages 100–110 are extrapolated by fitting a Gompertz line to ages 85–99; everyone is assumed dead by 111. The combined multiplier is capped at 12×: the proportional-hazards assumption has been tested to about 8× (three cardiometabolic diseases together) and nothing supports stacking further. A profile that hits the cap is told so.

Reading the numbers

Life expectancy is the mean age at death of people exactly like you; the median is the age half of them will have died by. They differ because the distribution is skewed — a small chance of dying young pulls the mean below the median.

"vs national average" compares you with a person of your age and sex in your country whose every factor is at the population average. The per-factor bars are computed by resetting one factor at a time to that average, holding the others as you entered them. Because the factors multiply, the bars do not add up exactly; the grey interaction bar is the difference, so the chart always reconciles to the headline figure. A fully low-risk profile lands 8–10 years above the average; that matches Li et al. (Circulation 2018), whose men with all five low-risk habits had a life expectancy of 87.6 at age 50 against a US average of about 79.

Parents. The evidence is about the age a parent reached, so a parent who is still living is credited with the ages they may still reach: the life table gives the chance of ending up in each band from their current age, and the expected hazard ratio is used. A mother alive at 75 will on average reach her mid-80s, so she counts as a mild advantage, not as "70s". "Unknown" means the population average and neither helps nor hurts. Grandparents are not modelled — family longevity clusters, but there is no well-measured effect independent of the parents to cite.

Mortality improvement. Death rates have fallen for a century. Off, the estimate assumes 2024's rates hold for the rest of your life — the "period" life expectancy quoted in the news, which understates how long today's adults will actually live. On, each future year's rate falls at the average annual pace the UN projects for your country, sex and age band over 2024–2073. It is a projection, not a promise.

What-if scenarios re-run the model with a change applied. Quitting smoking is modelled as it actually happens: the excess risk decays with a 7-year time constant to a 5% residual, so the benefit accrues over the years after quitting rather than arriving overnight (about 76% of the excess is gone after a decade, consistent with the 61–81% Jha et al. found for quitting in one's 40s).

Calibration checks

The pipeline is checked against published life-expectancy differences that the source studies report directly. These are computed live from this page's own engine:

Couples and the widowhood effect

  • Two linked lives. Each partner's death rate comes from the same engine, with both counted as partnered while both are alive. After one dies, the survivor's death rate is multiplied by the widowhood effect for their sex: 1.22 for men, 1.03 for women, against people still married (Moon et al. 2011, a meta-analysis of 15 cohorts and 2.3 million people).
  • Worst in the first months. The same meta-analysis finds 1.41 within six months of the loss and 1.14 after, against 1.12 overall, so the first year after the loss carries the average of those two, relative to the overall figure (×1.14).
  • Estimates for women vary. A study of 373,189 elderly US couples (Elwert & Christakis 2008) found 1.18 for men and 1.16 for women; the pooled 1.03 for women is the more conservative choice.
  • Not the "not partnered" factor. The model's 1.24 for being unpartnered pools never-married, divorced and widowed people; for a survivor the widowhood-specific figures are the better evidence, so the survivor keeps their partnered rate times the widowhood effect.
  • What is left out. Couples also share habits, money and environment, which links their deaths beyond grief. Describing your partner captures their own habits; nothing here models deaths you share (an accident, say), so "at least one of you alive" is still slightly optimistic. The independent version is one click away for comparison.

How much can 35 variables predict? — and the rules that keep them honest

The ceiling. For survival the relevant yardstick is not R² but discrimination — how well the model ranks who dies first (the C-statistic). Age and sex alone score about 0.75 for ten-year mortality; the best published multivariable tools (UK Biobank models, Canada's MPoRT) reach roughly 0.80–0.85; beyond that, individual mortality is genuinely unpredictable. The variables here were chosen for large, independent, well-replicated effects, which is what closes that gap. Adding correlated variables does not.

Optional measurements are judged against your peers. VO₂max, grip strength, waist, resting heart rate and hs-CRP are compared with age- and sex-specific norms, so a blank field (= population average) is exactly neutral and a value moves the estimate only by how far you sit from the norm. Measured fitness is what self-reported exercise was standing in for, so when VO₂max is entered the exercise answer counts at half weight.

Group caps. Correlated items are capped together so they cannot stack past what whole-pattern studies find: the five diet items at ±35% (diet-pattern studies find top-vs-bottom ≈ 0.75–0.8), education and income together at ±60%, the fitness group (exercise, VO₂max, grip, strength training, sitting, resting heart rate) at ±2.5× (Kodama's low-vs-high fitness alone is 1.70), and BMI + waist at ±3×.

The favourable side is soft. Combined low-risk profiles have been studied to about half the average hazard (Li 2018's five habits; Loef & Walach's meta-analysis). Below that point each further halving of risk counts as half, with a hard floor at one-fifth of average; the adverse side keeps its 12× cap, which multimorbidity data support to 8×. When either applies, the per-factor bars are computed without it and scaled to the headline, so they keep their relative sizes and still add up.

Deliberately left out, and why: total cholesterol (weak, U-shaped for all-cause mortality once treatment is common), height, vaping (no mortality data yet), active cancer (prognosis is disease-specific), religious attendance, optimism and "purpose in life" (large reported effects, but confounded and not causal), heart failure and dementia (a general calculator cannot represent their prognosis honestly), and grandparents (no measured effect independent of the parents).

Risk factors, hazard ratios and sources

HRs are all-cause mortality unless noted. "Reference distribution" is the population mix used to re-anchor the factor to the national average (see step 3). The blood-pressure HR is derived, not quoted: Lewington et al. report a two-fold vascular death rate per 20 mmHg of usual systolic pressure above 115; with vascular causes ≈ 28% of deaths in these countries (WHO), the all-cause multiplier is 1 + 0.28 × (2Δ/20 − 1). Alcohol HRs are back-solved from the life-expectancy losses at age 40 that Wood et al. report (≈ 6 months, 1–2 years, 4–5 years), so the engine reproduces the paper's own headline. The smoking split by intensity is interpolated between the light-smoker HR (Inoue-Choi) and the population-average current-smoker HR of 2.8 (Thun; Jha).

Limitations — read these

  • Not medical advice, not a prediction for you. It is the average outcome for a large group of people who share your answers. Individual outcomes vary enormously.
  • Factors overlap. Multiplying HRs from different studies assumes they are independent. Diabetes, blood pressure, BMI and inactivity are correlated, and residual confounding compounds when twenty estimates from twenty cohorts are multiplied. The group caps and the soft floor above are the guard, not a cure.
  • Self-rated health is a summary, not a cause. It earns its place because it predicts mortality after every diagnosis is accounted for, but changing the answer changes nothing in your body.
  • Mostly Western cohorts. Most HRs come from North American and European studies and are applied unchanged to every country. Only the baseline table and the smoking/BMI prevalences are country-specific.
  • Self-report. People under-report weight, drinking and smoking and over-report exercise. The estimate is only as honest as the inputs.
  • Lifestyle prevalence is not age-specific. Disease prevalence and blood pressure are age-dependent, but the smoking, weight, activity and social distributions are adult averages; among 85-year-olds there are fewer smokers (they have died or quit), so the re-anchoring is approximate at old ages.
  • Your habits are assumed to persist. Diseases you don't have are priced in at the population's onset rate, but the model does not have you start or stop smoking, gain weight or lose your social ties later in life — only the what-if panel changes those, and it changes them once, today.
  • Treated conditions. Enter your blood pressure as it is on treatment; the residual risk of treated hypertension is not separately modelled. Active cancer is not modelled at all — its prognosis is disease-specific.
  • Life tables lag. The baseline is 2024; with the improvement toggle off, no progress after that is assumed.
